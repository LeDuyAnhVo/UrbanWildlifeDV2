Urban Wildlife

From possums and lorikeets to blue-tongue lizards and butterflies, wildlife is part of everyday urban life. Between 2020 and 2024, people logged 16.6 million wildlife sightings across eight cities. This story looks at what those sightings tell us: who lives alongside us, how each city is different, and what happens to wildlife as our cities get busier.

Part 1

Eight cities, thousands of wild neighbours

Our capital cities are full of wildlife. Between 2020 and 2024, people spotted more than a thousand different species in every capital, and more than five thousand in Brisbane.

Part 2

Birds get noticed, insects make up the diversity

Birds are big, loud and out during the day, so birdwatchers log millions of sightings. Insects are small and hard to identify, yet that is where most of the variety is.

Part 3

How wildlife changes as citites grow

expect cities to record the most species, since that’s where most people are looking. That’s not quite what happens. When we compare cities with the outback, and busy suburbs with leafy ones, the city seems to push some animals out.

On average, council areas with about 30 people per km² have around 250 bird species Areas with about 3,000 people per km² have around 160
In Melbourne, outer areas with wetlands, coast or forest have the most bird species. Wyndham has 261, Mornington Peninsula 252 and Yarra Ranges 245. Busy inner suburbs such as Stonnington (114) and Glen Eira (127) have the fewest. Crowded areas also tend to be smaller, which explains part of the gap.

Part 4

Every city has its own wild character

Adaptable species turn up almost everywhere. But each capital also has its own locals that don’t make the list anywhere else.

Possums top Sydney’s list, koalas lead in Brisbane and flying foxes come first in Adelaide. In Darwin it’s the agile wallaby, and every animal in Hobart’s top five is native. But introduced animals are never far away. The red fox and the European rabbit each make the top 10 mammals in six capitals.

Part 5

When the city comes alive

Wildlife sightings follow the seasons. Reptiles and insects need warmth, so they mostly disappear in winter. Birds are seen all year round.

In Melbourne, October and November each bring about twice as many reptile sightings as an average month, while June and July bring only about a fifth. Insect sightings in December are more than ten times those in July.
Birds peak in October in every capital except Perth, where January is busiest. That’s spring breeding season, and also when BirdLife Australia runs its Aussie Backyard Bird Count. Try Darwin too. Its birds peak in the dry season from July to October, and its reptiles don’t slow down in winter at all.

Hobart’s reptiles almost vanish from May to August, then reach more than twice the average in December and January. Perth has a short, sharp season, with October bringing 3.4 times the sightings of an average month. Tropical Darwin has no winter break at all. Instead, its reptile sightings jump in July.
