Urban Wildlife

From possums and lorikeets to blue-tongue lizards and butterflies, wildlife is part of everyday urban life. Between 2020 and 2024, people logged 16.6 million wildlife sightings across eight cities. This story looks at what those sightings tell us: who lives alongside us, how each city is different, and what happens to wildlife as our cities get busier.

Part 1

Eight cities, thousands of wild neighbours

Our capital cities are full of wildlife. Between 2020 and 2024, people spotted more than a thousand different species in every capital, and more than five thousand in Brisbane.

Part 2

Birds get noticed, insects make up the diversity

Birds are big, loud and out during the day, so birdwatchers log millions of sightings. Insects are small and hard to identify, yet that is where most of the variety is.

Part 3

City versus bush: how biodiversity changes as cities grow

If cities attract the most observers, they should record the most species — unless the city itself is pushing some animals out. Comparing cities with the outback, and crowded suburbs with leafy ones, shows that it is.

On average, council areas with about 30 people per km² record around 250 bird species; areas with about 3,000 people per km² record around 160. Reptiles follow the same pattern (about 45 vs 20 species).
In Melbourne, outer areas with wetlands, coast or forest — Wyndham (261 bird species), Mornington Peninsula (252) and Yarra Ranges (245) — top the list, while dense inner suburbs such as Stonnington (114) and Glen Eira (127) record the fewest. Crowded areas are also smaller, which explains part of the gap.

Part 4

Every city has its own wild character

A handful of adaptable species are recorded almost everywhere, but each capital also has residents found nowhere else on this list.

Possums dominate Sydney's list, koalas Brisbane's, and the grey-headed flying-fox Adelaide's. In Darwin, agile wallabies top the list; in Hobart, the eastern barred bandicoot and the endangered Tasmanian devil both make the top seven. But introduced animals are never far away: the red fox and the European rabbit each appear in the top-10 mammals of six capitals.

Part 5

When the city comes alive

Wildlife sightings follow the seasons. Cold-blooded reptiles and insects disappear in winter, while birds are recorded all year.

In Melbourne, October and November each bring about twice the reptile records of an average month, while June and July bring only a fifth. Insect records in December are more than ten times those of July.
Birds peak in October in most capitals (Perth's peak is January) — spring breeding season, and also the month of BirdLife Australia's annual Aussie Backyard Bird Count. Try Darwin: its birds peak in the dry season (July–October) and its reptiles do not shut down in winter at all.

Hobart's reptiles are almost invisible from May to August, with records more than twice the average in December and January. Perth's reptile season is short and sharp — October records are 3.4 times an average month. In tropical Darwin there is no winter shutdown; instead, records spike in July.

Your backyard is part of the data

Our cities hold thousands of species, including threatened ones — but the most crowded suburbs record the fewest, and most of the variety (insects, reptiles) is barely watched. Protecting wetlands, creek corridors and bushland keeps wildlife in our cities. And every photo you upload to iNaturalist, or every bird you count in the Aussie Backyard Bird Count, ends up in the Atlas of Living Australia and helps scientists see what is changing.
